# Design psychology
```markdown
## Overview
This guideline explores various psychological principles and cognitive biases that influence human behavior and decision-making. Understanding these concepts allows designers to create more intuitive, engaging, and effective user experiences, while also avoiding common pitfalls in design and research.

## Usage Guidelines

### Guiding User Decisions
Leverage the following psychological principles to help users make choices efficiently and effectively:

*   **Hick’s law**: The time and the effort it takes to make a decision increases with the number of options. The more choices, the more time users take to make their decisions. *Minimize choices to streamline decision-making.*
*   **Default effect**: When given a choice between several options, the tendency to favour the default one. *Thoughtfully set defaults to guide users towards desired paths or recommended options.*
*   **Social proof**: In unfamiliar situations we tend to copy the actions of others. We can guide user decisions by demonstrating the activity of others, e.g. through testimonials or social activity. *Demonstrate user activity, e.g., through testimonials, ratings, or social shares, to build trust and guide decisions.*
*   **Centre-stage effect**: The centre stage effect is a phenomena that when presented with a number of choices people have a tendency to select the middle option. The centre stage effects works best if the differences between the options are simple and clearly communicated. *Consider placing a preferred option in the middle when differences between options are simple and clearly communicated.*
*   **IKEA effect**: The tendency for people to place a disproportionately high value on objects that they partially assembled themselves, such as furniture from IKEA, regardless of the quality of the end product. *Look for opportunities to involve users in co-creation or personalization, fostering a sense of ownership.*
*   **Anchoring**: The tendency for people to depend too heavily on an initial piece of information offered (considered to be the "anchor") when making decisions. Those objects near the anchor tend to be assimilated toward it and those further away tend to be displaced in the other direction. *Carefully consider the first piece of information presented, as it can influence subsequent perceptions and choices.*
*   **Mere exposure effect**: The tendency to prefer or like things merely because of familiarity with them. *Maintain consistency in design and patterns, making interfaces feel familiar and comfortable.*
*   **Gifting**: We feel the need to reciprocate when we receive a gift. *Consider small, thoughtful gestures or unexpected value to build goodwill and encourage engagement.*

### Creating Great Experiences
Design for memorable and positive user interactions by applying these principles:

*   **Aesthetic-usability effect**: Aesthetically pleasing designs are often perceived as being easier to use. We are more forgiving of attractive designs and assume they are easier to use. *Invest in visual design and polish to enhance perceived usability and user satisfaction.*
*   **Delighters**: We remember and respond favourably to small, unexpected and playful pleasures. This can range from amusing microcopy to solving a mental puzzle and help to form a favourable and memorable impression. *Incorporate amusing microcopy, solve mental puzzles, or add delightful animations to surprise and engage users.*
*   **Peak-end rule**: We judge our past experiences almost entirely by their peaks (pleasant and unpleasant) and how they ended. Consider this when designing user journeys and invest in creating pleasant peaks and ends. *Design user journeys to include pleasant peaks and strong, positive endings, particularly in critical flows.*
*   **Serial position effect**: We have much better recall of the first and last items within a list. If recall of specific items is critical, the most important items are at the beginning and end of the list. *Place the most important items at the beginning and end of lists or sequences where recall is critical.*
*   **Recognition over recall**: It’s easier to recognise things we have previously experienced than it is to recall them from memory. To promote recognition, make information and interface functions visible and easily accessible, allowing users to view choices one by one and recognise the relevant choice. *Make information and interface functions visible and easily accessible, allowing users to view choices and recognize the relevant one.*
*   **Pareto principle**: For many events, roughly 80% of the effects come from 20% of the causes. Focus the majority of effort on the areas that will bring the largest benefits to the most users. *Focus design and development effort on the features and areas that will bring the largest benefits to the most users.*
*   **Jacob’s law**: Users spend most of their time on other sites. This means that users prefer your site to work the same way as all the other sites they already know. Don’t reinvent the wheel unless there is a significant reason to do so. *Adhere to established design conventions and patterns. Avoid reinventing the wheel unless there's a significant user benefit.*
*   **Fitt’s law**: The time to acquire a target is a function of the distance to and size of the target. *Design interactive elements (buttons, links) to be large enough and easily reachable, especially on touch interfaces.*
*   **Zeigarnik Effect**: People remember uncompleted or interrupted tasks better than completed tasks. *Consider how to leverage this for onboarding or multi-step processes to encourage completion.*
*   **Doherty’s threshold**: Productivity soars when a computer and its users interact at a pace (<400ms) that ensures that neither has to wait on the other. *Optimize system performance and response times to keep users engaged and productive.*
*   **Von-Restorff Effect**: When users are presented with multiple similar objects, the one that differs from the rest will be remembered. A classic use case of this concept is the use of large, prominent CTA (calls to action) buttons. *Use visual distinction (e.g., size, color, prominence) to highlight key elements like Call To Action (CTA) buttons.*

## Best Practices

### Avoiding Research Mistakes
Be aware of and actively mitigate the following biases during user research and data interpretation:

*   **Observer-expectancy effect**: When a researcher expects a given result and therefore unconsciously manipulates an experiment or misinterprets data in order to find it. *Implement blind or double-blind studies where possible, and ensure diverse perspectives in data analysis.*
*   **Blind spot bias**: The tendency to see oneself as less biased than other people, or to be able to identify more cognitive biases in others than in oneself. *Practice self-awareness and actively seek feedback on your own potential biases.*
*   **Confirmation bias**: We like data that confirms our existing hypothesis/beliefs and discard those which challenge them. *Actively seek out data that challenges assumptions and consider alternative hypotheses.*
*   **Congruence bias**: The tendency to test hypotheses exclusively through direct testing, instead of testing possible alternative hypotheses. In an experiment, a subject will test their own usually naive hypothesis again and again instead of trying to disprove it. *Explore and test alternative solutions or hypotheses, rather than repeatedly validating a single idea.*
*   **Illusion of validity**: The tendency for people to overestimate their ability to interpret and predict accurately the outcome when analysing a set of data, in particular when the data analysed show a very consistent pattern—that is, when the data "tell" a coherent story. *Maintain a healthy skepticism, even with seemingly coherent data, and validate interpretations with diverse methods.*
*   **The false-consensus effect**: People tend to assume that others share their beliefs and will behave similarly in a given context. Only people who are very different from them would make different choices. *Avoid making assumptions about user behavior based solely on your own perspective. Conduct thorough user research with diverse participants.*

### Avoiding Bad Decisions
Guard against these cognitive biases that can lead to suboptimal design choices or user behavior:

*   **Sunk cost fallacy**: A general tendency to persevere with an option because of previously invested time or resources, regardless of whether this is the right choice. *Be willing to pivot or abandon initiatives that aren't working, despite past investments.*
*   **Bandwagon effect**: The tendency to do (or believe) things because many other people already do (or believe) the same. The bandwagon effect is characterised by the probability of individual adoption increasing with respect to the proportion who have already done so. *Be critical of trends and popular opinions; base design decisions on user needs and data, not just what others are doing.*
*   **Loss aversion**: We hate losing or letting go of what we have (even if more can be had). People have a tendency to prefer avoiding losses to acquiring equivalent gains: it is better to not lose $5 than to find $5. *Frame choices in terms of gains rather than losses where possible, and be mindful of how users perceive changes that might take away existing features or benefits.*
*   **Status quo bias**: The tendency to like things to stay relatively the same and be reluctant to any change. The current baseline (or status quo) is taken as a reference point, and any change from that baseline is perceived as a loss. *When introducing changes, clearly communicate benefits and minimize perceived loss. Provide easy ways to revert if possible.*
*   **Parkinson’s law**: Any task will inflate until all of the available time is spent. Avoid wasting time by timeboxing. *Implement timeboxing and strict deadlines to maintain focus and efficiency in design processes.*
```
